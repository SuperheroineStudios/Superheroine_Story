Chapter Sixteen — The Purple One

They walked her through the guts of the building like a delivery, and Rin catalogued every face she passed and hated all of them.

Green. Everywhere. Green streaks and green ink, Kyoya's little marks worn like team colors, a scatter of his soldiers loitering along the corridor and the loading floor, watching the cuffed girl go by with lazy, insolent stares. A couple of them smirked. One said something low to another and they both laughed.

Rin kept her face flat and let the fury run underneath it, cold and clean. She could take them. That was the maddening part — even cuffed, even now, she could put half these grinning chuckleheads on the floor before the other half remembered how to move. She could probably even make this so-called Viper work for it, on a fair day. And Kyoya — Kyoya wanted to face her with steel bracelets locking her wrists, wanted his big moment against a girl he'd made sure couldn't fight back. What a coward. All that theater, all that appetite, and he still stacked the deck.

Heroism, she thought bitterly, is going to be what kills me. She'd gone off a roof for a stranger. She'd walked into a dead lot for a woman in red. She'd stepped in front of a fist for Mei, and out of a café for Mei, and now here she was — handcuffed in a warehouse full of criminals on a sunny Sunday, all because she couldn't, ever, once, learn to just walk past the thing that needed her. Ryo had said it. It's not the money. You like the part where it's happening. He'd been right the whole time.

And Mei — the thought landed soft and sharp at once — Mei deserved better than the version of Rin that lied. Mei had run her fingers over a bruise and asked nothing. Mei had said it's a date and gone pink and meant it.

If there was an after — if — Mei deserved the truth. All of it.

The Viper stopped her at a set of heavy double doors.

"This is it," she said, and for a flicker there was that unreadable reluctance again, there and gone. "You go in alone. He wants it that way." She reached up, almost gently, and straightened Rin's collar where the cuffs had dragged it — an oddly human gesture from a woman who could throw a man across a street. "Good luck, kid. You'll want it."

Killed on a Sunday afternoon, Rin thought, staring at the doors. Surrounded by green idiots. In a sundress. How, exactly, did it come to this.

The doors opened.

She went in.
