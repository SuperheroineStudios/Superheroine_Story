Chapter One — Room 204

The room was smaller than her apartment and somehow felt larger, because half of it wasn't hers.

Rin set her second box down on the bare bed by the window and stood in the middle of Room 204, taking inventory the way she took inventory of everything — corners first, exits second, then the details. Two beds, two desks, two narrow wardrobes, a window that looked down onto a courtyard where the last cherry petals were giving up. The floor was pale wood, scuffed by years of students she'd never meet. Someone had left a thumbtack in the wall above the far desk, a single small wound in the plaster, and she found herself looking at it longer than it deserved.

The other half of the room was already claimed. Not with things — with intent. A folded quilt at the foot of the far bed, patterned with something small and repeating she couldn't make out from here. A row of sticky notes stuck to the edge of the far desk in a color she could only call aggressively cheerful. A half-unpacked box with a stuffed something poking out of the top, one felt ear bent against the cardboard flap.

Rin unpacked in fifteen minutes. Clothes into the wardrobe. Rice cooker on the shared shelf. The framed photo stayed face-down at the bottom of the duffel, and the duffel went under the bed, where she wouldn't have to decide about it.

She sat at her desk, unfolded the still-creased schedule, and started reading names of classes she'd never heard of, trying to feel like a person who belonged to a timetable.

The door opened.

"—so I told the front office the west stairs were faster but nobody ever listens to the girl who did the summer tour, and then I take the east stairs anyway because I'm an idiot, and—" A girl came through the doorway sideways, arms full, still finishing a sentence she'd apparently started in the hall to no one. "—oh."

She stopped.

She was small and soft-featured, dark hair pulled up into a bun that had clearly lost an argument with the day, strands escaping everywhere. Warm eyes. A cardigan a size too big with the sleeves shoved to the elbow. She was holding a laundry basket stacked with more sticky notes, a desk lamp, and the twin of the stuffed thing from the box.

And she was staring at Rin. Openly. Cardigan sleeves slipping back down her forearms, mouth slightly open, the flood of words just — gone.

Rin let her look. She was used to being looked at. She waited for the flinch, the recalibration, the little step people took back when they registered whatever it was about her that made rooms go careful.

It didn't come. The girl just kept staring, and then her ears went pink, and then she said, in a rush, "You're really pretty," and then looked absolutely horrified with herself.

Rin blinked.

"I mean—" The girl set the basket down too fast; a sticky-note pad slid off and she caught it against her knee. "That's not — hi. Hello. I forgot to — okay. Starting over." She straightened, took a breath, put out a hand that was still half-tangled in her cardigan sleeve, and gave a smile so genuine it was almost startling. "Mei. Mei Sasaki. I'm your roommate. Which you know, because — this is the room. Obviously. Wow, okay."

Rin looked at the offered hand for a beat. Then she took it. Mei's grip was warm and a little clumsy and she shook Rin's hand about one pump too many.

"Rin Aizawa."

"Rin." Mei repeated it like she was tasting it, then nodded to herself, satisfied. "Good name. Short. I like short. Mine's short too. We match." She winced. "That's a weird thing to say. I've been up since five, I'm not — I'm normally slightly more normal than this."

"It's fine," Rin said.

And the strange thing — the thing she'd turn over later that night, lying awake as usual — was that it was fine. The static that lived under every ordinary conversation, the smudged-glass feeling, hadn't shown up. Mei filled the quiet before it could form, and instead of grating, it was like being pulled into the shallow end of something warm. Rin found the corner of her mouth doing the small adjacent-to-a-smile thing it usually only did around Ryo.

She distrusted it immediately. And didn't stop it.

Mei unpacked like a weather event. She narrated it — this goes here, no, here, why did I bring three lamps — stuck a sticky note reading HYDRATE to the inside of the wardrobe door, hummed under her breath when she hit a snag with the quilt, and asked Rin a question roughly every ninety seconds without ever quite requiring an answer to keep going. Where was Rin from. Did she have a favorite class already. Was that a rice cooker, oh thank god, a person who cooks, the dining hall closed at eight and Mei was going to starve, she could already tell.

Rin answered the ones she wanted to and let the others drift, and Mei didn't seem to mind either way. It was, Rin realized somewhere in the second half-hour, the easiest she'd felt in a room in a long time.

Then the door opened again, without a knock, and Ryo leaned in.

"So," he said, to Rin, in the flat tone of a man delivering a grievance he'd rehearsed. "No text."

"I've been busy."

"You've been unpacking a rice cooker and one box of black clothes. I know exactly how busy you've been." He stepped in, hands in his pockets, tie a rumor, coffee can from this morning replaced by a coffee can from this afternoon. "So I came to find you, like I said I would, and now you have to hate it, like I said you—"

Mei stood up from behind the far bed.

"Oh! Hi!"

Ryo's whole sentence died in his mouth.

Mei crossed the room, sleeves flopping, and beamed up at him with the exact same undefended warmth she'd aimed at Rin twenty minutes ago. "You must be the person Rin was texting — no, wait, she wasn't texting you, that's why you're — okay, I'm Mei, I'm the roommate, are you a friend? You have to be a friend, Rin doesn't seem like she has a lot of—" She caught herself. "That came out mean. I meant it nice."

Ryo — who did not care what a single soul on this earth thought of him, who had walked through a courtyard full of first-years this morning like they were furniture — went visibly, catastrophically still.

Rin watched it happen with detached fascination. The tips of his ears. The way his jaw set, the specific casualness a person deploys when they are working very hard to be casual. He looked at Mei's outstretched hand like it was a live wire, took it for the briefest possible contact, and let go.

"Ryo," he said. One syllable. Clipped.

"Ryo," Mei said, delighted, oblivious. "Nice to—"

"I have to go." He was already turning. "Rin. Later. Text me. Or don't." He was at the door. "Don't."

And he was gone, the door easing shut behind him a little faster than a relaxed man would have shut it.

Mei stood holding the handshake that had ended too soon, her outstretched hand slowly lowering. She looked at the door. She looked at Rin. Her head tilted, a small crease appearing between her brows, and her voice dropped into something smaller.

"Did I— was it something I said? I do that. I say too much and then people—"

"It wasn't you," Rin said.

"Are you sure, because I have a real talent for—"

"Mei." Rin waited until the girl looked at her. "It's not you. It's him."

Mei's head tilted the other way, processing. Then something dawned across her face, slow and pleased, and she opened her mouth—

"Don't," Rin said, mildly, and Mei shut it, grinning behind pressed lips like she was holding a whole balloon of a thought captive.



"Okay but you have to let me show you around, because I did the summer tour and I know where everything is, which is genuinely the only useful thing about me," Mei said, already at the door, cardigan sleeves back down and shoved back up in a single practiced motion. "Also if I unpack one more box I'm going to lie down on the floor and become part of the building."

They walked.

Mei's tour was less a tour than a running commentary on her own life delivered against the backdrop of the school. This was the lecture hall where she'd get lost. That was the good vending machine, that was the evil vending machine that ate your money, avoid it, she was serious. The library, which she promised she'd use and they both knew she wouldn't. The long covered walkway between the humanities building and the courtyard, where the afternoon light came down slanted and gold through the gaps in the roof and lay across the stone in bars.

Rin listened with about half her attention and used the other half the way she always did — reading the space, the exits, the rhythm of a place she'd have to live in. It was pleasant. That was the word for it, and it was a word she didn't get to use much. Pleasant. The gold light, the small warm voice beside her, the low ordinary hum of a school settling into an evening.

Which was probably why she noticed the girl.

Across the courtyard, alone, silver hair catching the last of the sun — long, loose, almost too bright against the muted stone. She was standing near the far arcade with a bag over one shoulder, not going anywhere, just watching the courtyard the way Rin herself watched rooms. And on top of her head—

Rin stopped walking.

Ears. Two of them. Silver, tufted, angled — flicking once, unmistakably, toward a sound. Cat ears. Not clipped-on, not a headband. They moved.

Rin blinked.

Nothing. Just silver hair, a little windblown, and a girl standing under an archway. No ears. Nothing on her head but the light. Rin's pulse — the pulse she'd stopped noticing years ago — gave one hard, unfamiliar knock against her ribs, the way it only did in the dark before a fight.

The silver-haired girl had felt the look. Of course she had. Her head turned, and for a second their eyes met across the courtyard — blue, sharp, and annoyed, like Rin had caught her at something. The girl's expression pulled tight. She huffed, an audible little exhale of irritation, tossed the silver hair off her shoulder, and turned on her heel and walked away into the arcade's shadow without a backward glance.

"—Rin? Hello? You went somewhere." Mei was waving a sleeve-covered hand in front of her face. "You okay?"

Rin watched the empty archway a moment longer.

"Fine," she said. "Thought I saw someone I knew."

She didn't know why she lied. She filed the knock of her pulse away with everything else she didn't want to trace tonight, and let Mei tow her onward.



They ended up back at Room 204 as the courtyard lights came on below the window, small and yellow. Mei flopped onto her bed among her three lamps and her captive stuffed animal, and for a moment she didn't say anything at all, which was so out of character that Rin looked over.

"This is nice," Mei said, to the ceiling. Quieter than everything else she'd said all day. "Having someone. My last school—" She turned the stuffed thing over in her hands. "I wasn't good at it. Making people stay. I'd talk too much, or I'd get scared and not talk at all, and either way I'd end up eating lunch by myself doing this thing where I pretended I liked it." She looked over, suddenly shy of her own honesty. "So. This is nice. That's all. I'm glad it's you."

Rin sat with that. She wasn't good at the shape of things like this — didn't have the reflex for it, the automatic warm return. But she understood the thing underneath it better than Mei could know.

"I've got one person," she said, finally. "Ryo. That's it. Since I was fifteen." She turned her schedule over on the desk without reading it. "So. I get it. The pretending."

Mei's whole face went soft. She didn't say anything mushy, which Rin was grateful for — she just smiled, small and real, and hugged the stuffed animal, and let it be true between them.

"Classes tomorrow," Mei said eventually, a warning to herself. "I'm going to be so bad at mornings. You'll have to drag me. Sticky note me. I respond to sticky notes."

"I noticed."



Later, when the courtyard had gone quiet and Mei was drifting toward sleep in the other bed, Rin got up in the dark and dressed.

Not in a uniform. In black — the fitted jacket, the boots she could run in, the clothes that only felt like hers when something was trying to break her. She moved quietly, out of old habit, but not quietly enough.

"Mm—?" Mei surfaced, propped on one elbow, blinking at the shape of her roommate silhouetted against the window in full tactical black. Her sleep-slurred voice climbed with each guess. "Are you — wait. Are you going clubbing? You're going clubbing on a school night, oh my god you're so cool—" A beat. "Wait, no, that's not a clubbing outfit, that's a — are you a bodyguard? Do you have a second job? Is it a secret? Oh my god is it a secret—"

Rin paused at the door, one boot already in the hall.

"Night shift," she said. "Stocking. Convenience store two stops down. It pays the phone bill."

It was a boring enough lie to be believed, which was the whole art of it. Mei's face fell into sleepy sympathy.

"That's so sad," she mumbled, already sinking back into the pillow. "Bring back the good melon bread…"

"Sleep, Mei."

"Mm. Be safe. Sticky note…"

The dorm hall was empty. The stairwell was empty. Rin came out the west door into the night and stood a moment on the steps as the campus fell away behind her, all that pale stone and warm yellow light and the small sleeping ordinariness of it.

Ahead, past the gates, the city waited — the dusk not fully gone, the sky bruised violet over the rooftops, the first real dark pooling in the streets below. Somewhere down there was a job that needed to be done.

The static was already lifting. The edges were already sharpening.

Rin pulled her hood up, went down the steps, and walked toward the waiting dark like she was going home.
