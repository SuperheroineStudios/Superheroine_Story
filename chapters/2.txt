Chapter Two — Strays

The rooftops were the honest part of the city.

Rin moved across them the way most people moved down a sidewalk — without thinking about it, without hurrying, stepping the gaps between buildings where the fire escapes leaned close and dropping into a low run along the ridgelines where the roofs went flat and gravelled. Up here the neon didn't reach. Up here there was just the cold air off the water, the drone of the streets below, and the address the broker had texted her, glowing on her phone: an old residential block on the east edge, the kind of building that had been condemned twice and lived through it.

Her target lived there. A man named on the contract, a man who owed the wrong people, holed up in a walk-up he probably never left in daylight. Simple. She'd done a dozen like it.

She reached the last roof, crouched at the parapet, and looked down to place him before she went in.

He was already on the street.

Rin went still. The man from the contract — she knew the slope of his shoulders from the photo — was down on the sidewalk directly below, hood up, glancing over his shoulder every few steps like guilt had a rhythm. But he wasn't alone.

The man beside him was a problem.

Even from four floors up, in the wash of a dying streetlight, Rin could read him. He walked wrong — loose and heavy and certain, the walk of someone who'd never had to run from anything. A tattoo crawled up one side of his jaw and across his cheekbone, dark ink in a pattern she didn't recognize, and cutting through his dark hair was a single deliberate streak of green.

She'd never seen the man they called Kyoya. Nobody she knew had. But everyone in the trade knew his colors, and this was a calling card walking down a public street: the tattoo, the green. One of his.

Rin exhaled slowly through her nose. These aren't the ones you pick fights with, the brokers said, the few times they said anything about Kyoya at all. His people weren't muscle you rented by the night. They were the reason other muscle went home early.

She stayed on the roof and watched.

The two of them stopped at a shuttered storefront — a pharmacy, by the faded sign, dead for months behind its rolling steel grate. The tattooed man crouched at the lock like a man with all the time in the world, and the contract-target stood lookout badly, and Rin catalogued it with mild professional contempt. Typical. A break-in. Somebody's idea of collecting a debt in trade.

She'd wait them out. Take her mark after, alone, away from the green streak. That was the smart play, and Rin was, above everything else, smart about this.

Then the girl walked into the frame.

Rin's grip tightened on the cold concrete of the parapet.

Silver hair, unmistakable even at night, even from here — the girl from the courtyard, the one she'd told herself she'd imagined. Except now there was nothing to imagine. Two silver ears sat high on her head, sharp and alert and real, flattening as she took in the two men at the grate. And low behind her, sweeping once through the streetlight, a tail. A full, silver, deliberately-flicking tail.

So I didn't imagine it.

The knock came again in Rin's chest, that hard unfamiliar beat, and for once she didn't file it away. She just watched, because the cat girl was walking straight up to two of Kyoya's problem and planting herself between them and the grate like she had any business doing it.

Rin couldn't hear the words. She didn't need to. The girl's body language did the whole speech — squared shoulders, a pointed finger, a chin lifted in the specific way of someone who has decided, against all available evidence, that the right thing said loudly enough will fix a situation. The tattooed man straightened up from the lock. Tilted his head. Said something back that made the girl's ears go flat again.

Then it happened fast.

The cat girl moved first — and she was good, Rin gave her that instantly. Quick, springy, all that agility loaded into a spinning kick that cracked across the tattooed man's guard and drove him back two full steps into the grate with a rattle of steel. She followed it, light on her feet, a flurry of fast strikes that had the contract-target scrambling backward out of the way with his hands up.

But fast wasn't the same as finished.

Rin saw the mistake before it landed — the girl threw another high kick, beautiful, showy, and left the leg out there half a beat too long. The tattooed man had stopped being surprised. He caught it. Just closed one big hand around her ankle mid-air and held it, and Rin watched the whole fight change in his face, the certainty coming back.

He yanked. The girl's balance tore out from under her. He drove a forearm into her chest as she came off-balance and sent her staggering back across the sidewalk, ears splayed, arms windmilling, until she caught herself against a parked car with a jolt that had to have hurt.

Rin was already moving.

She didn't decide to. That was the part she'd examine later and dislike — there was no decision, no cost-benefit, no smart play. Her body was over the parapet and onto the fire escape before the thought finished forming, dropping level to level in the practiced controlled falls she'd built her whole night-life on, boots ringing off the iron. The last drop put her in the alley mouth. Three fast strides put her on the street.

The tattooed man was advancing on the staggered girl, unhurried, cracking his neck.

Rin stepped into the space between them and put a kick into his chest.

It wasn't showy. It was a straight thrusting teep with her whole weight behind it, driven from the hip, and it caught him center-mass and folded him backward off his feet entirely. He hit the shuttered grate with a boom that rolled down the empty street and slid to the pavement, wheezing.

Silence, for a second. The cat girl stared up at her from against the car, blue eyes huge.

Rin didn't look away from the threat. But she felt the girl's eyes on her, and the two of them held it for a moment across the sudden quiet — an eye-to-eye that said everything a handshake would have and none of the words.

The tattooed man ruined it by getting up.

Faster than he had any right to. He came off the grate low and hard, taking her surprise for granted, swinging a heavy hooking punch meant to end things. Rin slipped it — turned her head, felt the wind of his knuckles pass her cheek — and answered with a cross down the center that snapped his head sideways. Snap-crack. She didn't stop. She stepped his blind side and dug a hook into his short ribs, felt it land deep, heard the hff punch out of him. He was good. He ate it and threw back, and this was the difference between him and the last four men she'd folded in a warehouse: he was still fighting when he should have been falling.

He got one in. She pressed a beat too eager and he buried a fist in her stomach — a real one, weight behind it, and the air went out of her in a hard silent rush and the world narrowed for a half-second to just the burn under her ribs. Her hand dropped an inch. Old instinct. She stepped off-angle and hid it, breathing through the hitch, letting nothing show on her face except the thing that always showed instead: she went quiet. Colder. The way that meant the fight was almost over for someone, and it wasn't going to be her.

She let him commit again. He did. And when he did, she was already gone off the line, pivoting through the miss into a spinning elbow that caught him clean behind the ear.

Thwock.

He went down like the strings had been cut, and stayed down.

Rin stood over him one breath, wiping a thumb across the corner of her mouth. It came away clean. Fine. She turned, walked to the contract-target — who had spent the whole fight pressed flat against the pharmacy grate praying to become a wall — took a fistful of his collar, hauled him in, and folded him with a single short punch to the jaw. He crumpled beside the other one, and that was the whole tally: one gut shot she'd feel tomorrow, one job done.

She pulled out her phone and called it in. Location. Two packages, one contracted, one a bonus Endo could sell up the chain to whoever wanted a piece of Kyoya's crew. Flat voice. Business.

Then she hung up, and finally looked at the cat girl.

The girl had picked herself up off the car and was staring at Rin like she'd watched a magic trick she wanted explained. Her ears had come back up, forward now, fully interested. She limped a step closer, tail swishing, and looked Rin up and down — openly, head to boots and back, the way you'd size up a rival or a really nice motorcycle.

"Okay," she said, with feeling. "Okay, that was — who are you? That elbow thing. The— " She mimed a clumsy little spin and nearly lost her balance again. "You didn't even say anything. You just did it. That's so cool it's kind of rude, honestly."

Rin said nothing, which the girl took as encouragement.

"I had it, by the way," she added, chin coming up. "Under control. The whole— that was a strategic staggering." She winced and pressed a hand to her side. "But thank you. For the— assist. I'm not too proud to say thank you. My mom raised me better than the average person, which is why I was over here in the first place, because those two were going to rob a pharmacy, an actual pharmacy, people need medicine from there and they were just going to—" She cut herself off, took a breath. "Sorry. It makes me mad. When people do wrong things and just— get to. I can't watch it. I've never been able to just watch it."

She said it with total conviction, this drenched-in-conviction sincerity that Rin found she couldn't quite mock. It was the same thing she saw sometimes in Ryo, the thing that lived under his slouch. A sense of should.

"You're bleeding on your ear," Rin said.

The girl clapped a hand to it, checked, found nothing, glared. "I am not."

"Made you check."

The girl huffed — an actual huff, ears twitching — and then, deciding to be charmed instead of annoyed, stuck out a hand. "Yuki. Yuki Kisaragi." A pause, then, brightly, like it was a wonderful coincidence and not the thing that would tie the whole rest of it together: "Hey — you go to the academy, right? The one on the west hill? I saw you in the courtyard. You looked at me like—" She waved it off. "Whatever. Point is we're gonna be at the same school! Which means— okay, hear me out. You could teach me. Or we spar. We learn off each other. You've clearly got the— " another clumsy spin-mime " —and I've got, um, enthusiasm, and reach, and—"

"No," Rin said.

Yuki blinked. "No?"

"I don't take strays." She was already turning, hood coming back up, the job behind her and the long roof-route home ahead. "Get that side looked at. You dropped your guard on the third kick. That's why he caught it."

She left Yuki standing on the empty street under the dead pharmacy sign.

Behind her, she heard the huff. Louder this time, indignant, the sound of a girl who had just been given a critique she hadn't asked for and was going to think about for a week. And then, called after her, half furious and half something else entirely:

"Fine! But I'm gonna learn it anyway! Watch me! Whatever it takes, I'm gonna—"

The rest was lost as Rin took the first fire escape up, back into the honest dark, the corner of her mouth doing that small treacherous thing again.



Room 204 was dark and warm when she slipped back in.

Mei was fast asleep in the far bed, curled around the stuffed animal, one sticky note peeling loose from the wardrobe door in the draft from the window — HYDRATE, going soft at the corner. Her breathing was slow and untroubled. She'd never know Rin had been gone at all.

Rin peeled out of the black in the dark, easing every drawer shut, and lay down on her half of the room with the ache blooming quietly under her ribs where the gut shot had landed. She stared at the ceiling and the single thumbtack still in the plaster, and let the night replay.

The green streak. The tail that was real. The stray she'd told herself she wouldn't collect and had already, apparently, started teaching.

She sighed into the dark.

She'd have to tell Ryo. He'd want to know she'd tangled with one of Kyoya's, and he was owed that, the way he was owed all her true things. But he'd hear the rest of it too — the part where she hadn't waited for the smart play, where she'd gone over a parapet for a girl she didn't know because someone was getting hurt. And he'd get that look. The one that was almost a smile. There she is, the look said. The hero she keeps swearing she isn't.

She really did try not to play that part.

She closed her eyes and, for once, her pulse was still loud enough to keep her company as she drifted off.
