Chapter Eleven — The Viper

Yuki pressed herself into the cold gravel and did not move, and — thanks to the two silver advantages on top of her head — she didn't have to move to hear every word.

The woman in green crossed the alley toward the three frozen thugs with the unhurried ease of someone arriving at a meeting she'd scheduled. One of the men found his voice, and it came out cracked down the middle.

"Th-the Viper—"

So that was her name, or the one the dark had given her. Yuki filed it, ears swiveling.

"I'm afraid so." The Viper stopped a comfortable distance from them, hands loose at her sides, and inclined her head in something that was almost, mockingly, an apology. "And I really am sorry about this. It isn't personal. Boss's orders — this whole block's been earmarked, and you three are standing on a number I'm supposed to bring home tonight."

"We didn't—we just—" The cracked-voice one was backing up, crowbar forgotten in his grip. "We heard the same rumors as everyone, okay? Easy pickings, nobody's watching this part of town anymore. I just needed fast cash, I swear, I didn't know it was your—"

"Everyone needs fast cash." Her voice stayed pleasant. "That's rather the problem with rumors."

Her eyes flared green.

Not a trick of the headlights — a real, sourceless brightening, the green going vivid and lit-from-within, and every hair on Yuki's tail stood straight up.

One of the men — braver or stupider than the rest — swung. A full pipe-swing, committed, aimed at her head.

He had no chance. None.

The Viper tilted out of its path without seeming to hurry, stepped inside his reach, and dropped him with a single edge-of-the-hand chop to the side of the neck — one hand, one strike, and he folded straight down like his skeleton had been quietly removed. Before the other two could do more than flinch, she was already moving. She backhanded the second aside with a chopping blow that sent him sprawling into the wall like something discarded, and pivoted through a wheel kick that caught the third across the chest and launched him a full body-length down the alley to land in a heap of limbs and dropped crowbar.

Three seconds. Maybe less.

She moves like Faith did, Yuki thought, and her mouth had gone dry. The same skipped-frame speed. The same casual rewriting of what a body should be able to do to another body. That impossible tier, the one Rin had stood dazed at the bottom of two nights ago — here it was again, in green this time.

And Yuki — who had crouched on this roof so sure, who'd run the whole plan in her head, drop-behind-the-far-one, take-him-quiet — felt the certainty drain right out of her. She couldn't touch that. Not on her best night, not with a month of Rin's coaching, not even close. Her ears flattened miserably against her skull.

Where's Rin when you actually need her, she thought, and hated how small it sounded even inside her own head.

The Viper lifted a hand to her ear — a radio — and said something short into it.

And then, in a display of confidence that bordered on rude, a semi truck backed into the alley. Beeping. Its reverse lights washing the walls. Subtle as a marching band. Yuki watched it come, boggling, because it was the middle of the night in a city supposedly crawling with crime, and these people were rolling up a full cargo hauler to a break-in like they'd filed paperwork for it.

It was like they didn't care about the police at all.

But, Yuki thought slowly, remembering something Rin had told her — a woman in red, a line in the city between the trouble that got a response and the trouble that got buried — why would they.

A small crew poured out of the truck's cab and the jeep both — and these ones were different. Marked. Green streaks, hard ink, the coiled competent quality the freelancers had lacked. Kyoya's actual people. They took over the door the freelancers had started, and where the three thugs had been clumsy and slow, this crew was a machine. They peeled the storage building open unit by unit and stripped it with brutal efficiency, a chain of hands passing whatever had value up into the truck's belly. Whatever had been locked away in there — someone's inventory, someone's savings, someone's something — it was simply gone, twenty minutes start to finish, and then the truck's door rolled down and it pulled away into the night as calmly as it had arrived.

And Yuki, watching, noticed a strange thing through the fear.

The three freelancers were gone. Not dead — gone. At some point during the loading they'd dragged themselves up and stumbled off into the dark on their own two feet, groaning, clutching ribs and necks, but alive. The Viper had dropped all three of them and hadn't killed a single one. Every strike had been placed to disable, not to end. For all the terror she radiated, she'd chosen, deliberately, to leave them breathing.

Yuki didn't know what to do with that. Mercy and monstrosity in the same three seconds.

The crew loaded up. The truck left. And then it was just the Viper again, alone in the emptied alley, walking back to her jeep.

She stopped at the driver's door. And turned her head, and looked — directly, unerringly, without a moment of searching — up at the exact lip of the exact roof where Yuki was flattened into the gravel, and met her eyes.

Yuki's stomach dropped through the building.

Of course, she thought, despairing. Of course she knew. She knew the whole time. The whole prowl, the careful roof-to-roof, the held breath — the Viper had clocked her before she'd finished climbing, the same way Faith had once found her behind a dumpster, the way apparently everyone in this terrifying new world could find her while she thought she was invisible.

The Viper didn't come for her. Didn't call out, didn't send the crew, didn't so much as change her stance.

She just smiled — small, knowing, almost amused — and got into her jeep, and drove away, leaving Yuki alive on a rooftop with the very clear understanding that it was a choice, and that it had been hers to make.

"…Okay," Yuki breathed, unpeeling herself from the gravel with shaking hands. "My hiding is terrible. Everyone keeps saying it and I keep — okay. Okay. Not the point right now."

She was more scared than she was annoyed, which was a first, and she didn't waste the fear. She went back to campus the long way, doubling her route, cutting through places even she wasn't sure of, checking over her shoulder the entire time for headlights that never came.
