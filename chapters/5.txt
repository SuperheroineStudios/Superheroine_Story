Chapter Five — Dormant

Ryo found her first, which meant the teasing came before the coffee did.

He was waiting outside the lecture hall when the first class let out, propped against the wall with the boneless patience of a man who had somewhere to be and had decided against it. He had Rin's texts from earlier on his mind. He didn't say anything until she was close enough that only she'd hear it.

"So," he said. "You saved somebody again."

"I did a job."

"You went off a roof for a woman getting jumped by five guys." He pushed off the wall and fell into step. "That's not a job. That's a hobby. You've got a hobby now, and it's rescuing people, and it keeps almost getting you killed. I want that on the record."

"I had a contract in that lot. The mark was standing right there in the middle of it." Rin heard her own voice climb into something defensive and hated it. "I was going to have to go through those men either way. The bounty was the point."

"Uh-huh."

"It was."

"Rin." He looked sideways at her, dry and knowing, that buried steadiness surfacing under the slouch. "You could've waited them out. Taken the mark after, in the quiet, the way you always do. You didn't wait. You never wait when somebody's actually getting hurt in front of you." He shrugged. "It's not the money and it's not the bounty. You like the part where it's happening. You always have. I stopped arguing about it, I just don't let you pretend I don't see it."

She huffed—an actual exhale through the nose, short and irritated, and she felt the childishness of it and did it anyway.

"There she—" Ryo started.

"Don't finish that."

He didn't. He just smiled into his coffee, insufferable.

"You two are so married," said Yuki, arriving.

Ryo went rigid mid-sip. It was becoming a reliable law of the universe: Yuki entered a ten-foot radius and Ryo's spine turned to rebar. But this time—Rin noted it with quiet interest—he didn't leave. He stayed put, jaw set in that studied casualness, ears already lost, but rooted to the spot like retreating would prove something.

"We are not," Rin said.

"You bicker like it. It's cute." Yuki waved the whole thing off, but her attention wasn't really on the joke; her ears—present today, Rin checked, because she always checked now—were tipped forward with a keener curiosity. "Okay but I've been dying since first period. The woman. Red lady. You saw her again, didn't you, I can tell, you've got a face."

"I have a face."

"A specific face. Spill."

So Rin gave them the short version. The dead lot, the circle of muscle, the woman in the center taking a beating that turned out to be a performance, the reveal, the handshake. "Her name's Faith Kiryu. She's an investigator—a real one, and a good one. She let those men land hits just to see whether I'd come off the roof. It was a test." She paused. "I failed it by passing it."

Yuki's eyes had gone huge and shining. "She took actual hits to run a character test? That's—" She pressed both hands to her chest. "That's the coolest thing I've ever heard. I'm in love. I've never met her and I'm in love. Does she need an apprentice. Do you think she'd—"

"She's dangerous," Rin said. "Don't fall for the red."

"Too late, it's a whole thing now."

Ryo, who had been quiet, spoke up—and when he did, the slacker was somewhere underneath it. "I didn't figure this school for having other fighters in it." He said it lightly, but his eyes moved, deliberate, from Rin to Yuki and back. "First Rin, sneaking out at night to do—whatever this is. Now you show up out of nowhere, and Rin says the man you fought the other day was one of Kyoya's people." A beat. "That's a lot of coincidence for one small campus."

Yuki's ears flattened. The bravado dropped out of her all at once, and something honest and slightly panicked took its place. "It's—okay, it's not what it looks like. I'm not, like, in anything. I don't have a broker or a contract or a cool red outfit." She fidgeted with the strap of her bag. "I just— I can't stand it. When people do bad things to people who can't stop them. It gets under my skin and it stays there and I can't walk past it, I've never been able to walk past it, even when I know I should. So I don't sneak out to make money. I sneak out because I saw two guys about to rob a pharmacy and my legs were already moving before my brain voted." She looked at the floor. "That's all it is. I promise."

The teasing was gone from the group entirely. Ryo studied her for a long moment—the read Rin had watched him give a hundred marks and a few friends—and then, slowly, he nodded.

"Yeah," he said, quiet. "I get that. Not being able to walk past it." He didn't look at Rin when he said it, which was how she knew he partly meant her. "That's a good way to be. Dangerous, but good."

Yuki looked up, surprised, and then a smile broke over her like sun coming through—relieved, pleased, the whole earnest engine of her lighting back up. "Yeah?"

"Yeah."

Rin felt, abruptly, like a third wall in a two-person room. There was a thread running between the grumpy boy and the cat girl that had nothing to do with her, and she had the sudden odd instinct to step out of its path.

"My next contract's in two days," she said, mostly to have something to say. "Faith told me we'll probably keep crossing paths—same hours, same targets. If Kyoya's people are turning up on my jobs, I'd rather not walk into that alone." She glanced at Yuki. "You want in? You'd have to actually keep your guard up on the third kick this time."

"I knew you'd bring up the kick—" Yuki was already nodding, delighted. "Yes. Absolutely yes. Partners. Not-strays. Whatever you're comfortable calling it."

"I'm calling it you keep your guard up."

Ryo's brow had furrowed. "You're taking her on a job. Against people who put a professional down with a pipe."

"I'll be fine," Yuki started.

"I'm asking Rin."

"She'll be fine," Rin said, and meant it, and was a little surprised to find she did. "She's good, Ryo. Rough, but good—fast, and she doesn't scare. The one I fought that night was exceptionally strong, that's a different tier. Regular muscle, she can handle. And I'd rather have her watching my back than watching a pharmacy alone at two in the morning." She held his eyes. "I won't let anything happen to her."

Ryo looked at her a moment longer, the worry not gone but folded down, tucked into the place where he kept everything he couldn't argue her out of. "Okay," he said. "But you text me. Both nights. Before and after."

"Maybe."

"Rin."

The bell saved her.



Liliana didn't pretend anymore.

Rin walked into the lecture hall and the woman's gaze found her at the door and stayed, tracking her all the way to her seat, unhurried and unhidden. And there was a smile on her mouth—small, precise, meant to be seen. For one strange half-second Rin thought she caught something underneath it that didn't belong on a teacher's face at all, a cold flat glitter she could only have called appetite, the look of someone regarding a thing they intended to have.

Then it was just a professor smiling at a promising student, and Rin decided she'd imagined it, because the alternative made no sense, and sat down.

The lecture proceeded as normally as anything involving Liliana could. She dismantled three students with questions that had hooks in them, moved through the material with that languid, cat-at-play precision—and she kept coming back to Rin. More than to anyone. A question, then another, then a third aimed like a dart, each one a little sharper, as if she were probing for where the resistance was, testing the give of something. Rin answered them all, flatly, correctly, and felt each answer land somewhere and get filed.

"She's obsessed with you," Yuki whispered afterward, in the corridor, hushed and thrilled. "It's not even subtle. And—okay, don't laugh—when she was looking at you? I could've sworn her eyes went red. Just for a second. Like a flash." She shivered. "I probably imagined it. It's a weird school. The lighting's weird."

"Yeah," she said dryly. "They probably did."

Yuki barked a laugh. "Right? Red-eyed murder-teacher. Very normal. Love it here." And they were both laughing, the real kind, the kind Rin didn't get much practice at, all the way to the point where they had to split for different halls.



Ryo wasn't at the coffee machine.

Rin registered the small absence—he was always at the coffee machine, it was practically his second address—and let it go, and turned toward the biology wing instead. She found Mei halfway there, or rather Mei found her, materializing at her elbow with a whole-armed wave and the specific brightness of a person who'd been counting down to exactly this.

"There you are, I've been alone with strangers for hours," Mei announced, tragic. "Do you know how many classes I sat through without a single person to whisper to? I had thoughts and nowhere to put them. I nearly perished."

"You survived."

"Barely. Emotionally I'm in ruins. Walk me to bio, restore my will to live."



Vomi's class was different this time, and Rin felt the difference the way she felt weather changing.

The professor moved to the front with her lab coat open over red and blue, auburn hair loose, and she didn't open with a warning today—she opened with a question none of them could answer, and then spent ninety minutes answering it herself in that low velvet voice that made a biology lecture feel like being let in on something.

She went deep. Down past cells, past the tidy diagrams in the textbook, into the machinery underneath—atoms, bonds, the quiet arithmetic that decided what a body could and couldn't do. And then, almost offhand, she turned it somewhere stranger.

"There is a theory," Vomi said, trailing a hand along the edge of a lab bench, "and I stress that it remains only a theory—that in certain rare individuals, buried in that arithmetic, there sits a gene that does nothing. Dormant. Silent for a whole life, in most. But under the right conditions—the theory suggests it could wake." She let the word sit. "And that a body running that gene might do things a body shouldn't. Move faster. Endure more. Hear a heartbeat across a room. Think three steps ahead of where thinking should be able to reach. Reserves the rest of us simply do not have access to." A small, precise smile. "Science fiction, most would say. And most would be comfortable saying it."

Mei was writing so fast her pen was smoking, whispering this is so cool under her breath.

Rin, on the other hand, had checked out somewhere around only a theory. It wasn't that she was lazy—she was rarely lazy—but there was a line past which a subject stopped being useful to her and became a story someone was telling, and dormant magic genes were three towns past that line. She let her attention drift out the high windows to the gold afternoon and the courtyard below and did not, in any meaningful sense, take a single note.

When the bell rang and the room began to empty, Vomi's voice cut softly through the shuffle.

"Miss Aizawa. A moment."

Mei glanced over, curious, but a knot of students carried her toward the door and she didn't hear, and Rin let her go with a small nod. She walked up to the front of the room as it emptied out around her, until it was just the two of them and the smell of antiseptic and the dust turning in the window-light.

"We haven't been introduced," Vomi said, in a register entirely different from the one she used on a full room—lower, warmer, stripped of the performance. "Not properly. Karin Vomi." She inclined her head. "Most only ever hear the surname. You may as well have the whole of it."

"Rin Aizawa."

"I know." A pause, gently amused. "You weren't listening today."

Rin didn't flinch. "I was listening."

"You were looking at the courtyard. Around the twenty-minute mark you stopped even pretending." Vomi tilted her head, and there was no anger in it, only a kind of patient reading, the same measuring look she'd given Rin across the emptying room days ago. "It's all right. I'm not scolding. I'm simply noting that I don't often bore people who can't afford to be bored."

"I wasn't—"

"You were." Said kindly, and with total certainty, and Rin gave up the denial because it was clearly costing her more than it was worth.

"Sci-fi theories bore me," Rin admitted. "Dormant genes. Hidden power. It's a good story. It's not a thing I can use."

Vomi studied her for a long moment. "I see a great deal of potential in you, Miss Aizawa," she said finally. "More than in most of the bright, well-behaved girls who fill their notebooks. And I'd suggest—only suggest—that you take these particular lessons more seriously than you're inclined to." Her smile returned, small and precise and impossible to read. "You might find they concern you more directly than you expect."

"I'm not really the type to space out in class," Rin said, which was true. "Sci-fi just isn't where I live."

"No?" Vomi turned back toward her desk, gathering her things with unhurried grace. "A great deal that we now take for fact began as fiction someone was too skeptical to sit through. But I've kept you long enough." A glance back, over her shoulder, brown eyes level. "Go on. Your friend's waiting. And do think about it."

Rin went.



Mei was hovering just past the door, vibrating with restraint.

"What was that," she demanded the instant Rin cleared the threshold, falling into step. "She kept you after. Professor Scary-Pretty kept you after. Are you in trouble? Are you her favorite? Both? Is it a both situation?"

"I don't know," Rin said, and found she meant it precisely. "She said I have potential. Told me to take her class more seriously." She frowned faintly at the middle distance. "It felt like it was about more than biology. I couldn't tell you what."

"Ominous," Mei breathed, delighted and unbothered, because to Mei the whole world was a series of interesting rooms. "You collect them, you know. Mysterious intense people who fixate on you. It's a real pattern. First the grumpy one, now the scary-teacher—"

"She's not a scary-teacher."

"She has scary-teacher energy, that's different, and you didn't deny the fixating—"



The night was ordinary, which Rin was learning to appreciate as its own small luxury.

She and Mei ate too-late noodles cross-legged on the floor between their beds, and Mei re-told the dormant-gene lecture with theatrical hand gestures and at least forty percent invented details, and added a fresh sticky note to her wall for reasons Rin didn't fully follow but decided not to question. The little constellation of Rin's excuses had a neighbor now: "kept after class by ominous hot professor — REAL, witnessed."

Later, when the lamp was off and Mei's breathing had gone slow and even, Rin's phone lit the dark.

ryo: home safe? ryo: (this counts as the after-class text. you're welcome. i lowered the bar for you.)

She almost smiled.

rin: safe. class was normal. mostly. ryo: mostly is doing a lot of work in that sentence rin: talk tomorrow. ryo: night. don't do anything i wouldn't do. rin: you wouldn't do anything. ryo: exactly. good system.

She set the phone face-down on her chest and lay looking at the ceiling, at the single old thumbtack still lodged in the plaster above the far desk.

Two days. Her next contract was in two days, and somewhere out in the city Faith Kiryu was working the same dark, following the same money up the same chain, and would—she'd said so herself—turn up again. Same hours. Same targets. And Rin noticed, with a small clinical alarm, that the thought didn't sit in her like a complication.

It sat in her like something to look forward to.

Which was strange. She turned it over, the way she turned over anything she didn't trust—the low, unfamiliar eagerness of wanting to run into a person, of hoping the dark would hand her a particular face. She hadn't wanted to run into anyone in a long time. She wasn't sure she liked how much she wanted to run into this one.

She closed her eyes on it, and didn't solve it, and let the ordinary dark carry her under.
