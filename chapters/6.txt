Chapter Six — Crimson

The last bell of the week had a different sound to it, or maybe that was just the way everyone else heard it — the corridors emptying fast and loud, girls spilling toward a free evening. Rin walked it at her own pace, Mei orbiting beside her, and did not feel the relief the rest of them clearly felt, because for her the week didn't end at that bell. It only handed her off to the other job.

"You're doing the thing," Mei said, halfway across the courtyard.

"What thing."

"The eager thing. You've got it all day." Mei studied her sidelong, that quick reading she did under all the chatter. "You walk different when you know you're going out later. Lighter. Like a dog that heard the leash." She grinned. "You're going out tonight. Aren't you."

Rin gave her a look. Not a confirmation, not a denial — just a flat, level look held one beat too long.

"Yeah," Mei said softly, answering herself, the grin gentling. "Okay. Just come back."

She didn't push. She'd learned not to, and Rin had noticed how much it cost her not to, which somehow made it matter more.

Later, in the amber wind-down of the dorm, Rin dressed for the dark.

Mei watched from her nest of lamps and quilt, chin on her knees. "You know," she mused, as Rin pulled the fitted jacket on, "for restocking convenience-store shelves, you dress really good. Suspiciously good. Nobody's putting instant noodles on an endcap looking like that." She started to unfold herself off the bed, reaching, "here, your collar's—"

Rin fixed it herself. One clean tug, done, before Mei's hands got there.

Mei's arms lowered, and her whole face fell into a small theatrical pout. "You took my job. That was my job. I have one job."

"It was folded under."

"I would've fixed it." She flopped back, wounded, then propped herself up on an elbow with a new gleam. "I could come, you know. Whatever it is. I'm very helpful. I hold things. I'm morale."

"You wouldn't like it."

"You don't know that. Maybe I'm secretly hardcore."

"You screamed at a moth on Tuesday."

"That moth was aggressive and you weren't there to—"

A knock at the door cut her off.

Rin opened it to Yuki, dressed for the night herself — dark clothes, hair pulled back, silver ears up and alert, practically bouncing on the balls of her feet with pre-mission energy. "Ready when you—" Yuki started, then her gaze slid past Rin into the room and locked onto Mei, and everything else evaporated. "Oh. Hi. Who are you."

"That's Mei," Rin said. "My roommate. Mei, Yuki."

It took approximately four seconds.

"Your ears are real," Mei breathed, already off the bed and across the room, hands hovering like she wanted to touch and knew she shouldn't. "They're real, they moved, oh my god, hi, I'm Mei, you're beautiful, is that rude? I don't care, you're beautiful. Rin can we keep her—."

"Thank you, finally someone with taste—" Yuki was beaming, ears forward, instantly, helplessly delighted. "I like her. Rin, I like her. Your roommate has excellent energy, this is the warmest person I've ever—"

"We're going to be late!" Rin said.

"—do you want to get food sometime, I feel like we'd get food really well together—"

"Yuki."

"—okay but after, Mei, we're getting food after—"

Rin took Yuki by the collar and physically redirected her toward the hall. Yuki went, still talking, walking backward, waving, and Mei waved back with her whole arm from the doorway, delighted, calling something about melon bread that neither of them fully caught.

"You," Rin said, once they were down the stairwell and into the cooling evening, "fall for people too easily."

"I do not!"

"You've known her for ninety seconds. You proposed dinner twice."

"That's not falling, that's recognizing quality—" Yuki huffed, ears flicking. "There's a difference."

"Mm." Rin let a beat pass, then, dry: "Just don't fall for me. I'd have to kick you."

Yuki sputtered, went pink, recovered. "In your dreams, boss. You're way too grumpy. I have standards."

"You just said you'd get dinner with a stranger's roommate on sight."

"Different context—"

The broker's details came through as they crossed into the older part of the city, the neon thinning, the streets narrowing. Rin read the packet on her phone — a name, a face, a last-known spot, the usual — and stopped walking.

The photo showed a man with dark hair. And through it, a single deliberate stripe of green.

"Great," she muttered.

Yuki leaned in to look. "What? What's—oh." Her ears pinned. "That's the—the tattoo-green-streak guys. Kyoya's people." Her jaw set, that stubborn earnest fire kindling. "Good. I'm not losing this time. I've been practicing the—" she did the spin-mime, badly, on a public sidewalk. "I've got it now. Mostly."

Rin exhaled through her nose. Of course. Of course tonight's mark wore the green. The contracts had been drifting that direction all week, brokers quietly funneling her toward Kyoya's crew like water finding a channel — and now she understood, a little better, why a certain woman in red had been so interested in the girl who kept turning up on those jobs. Faith wasn't following Rin. They were both following the same current up the same river.

"No wonder she—" Rin started.

Hands closed on both their arms and yanked them sideways into a black slot between two buildings.

Yuki yelped. Rin's body was already moving to break the grip when a low, smooth voice said, close to her ear, "It's me. Quietly, if you can manage it."

Faith.

She released them and stepped back into the alley's gloom, red gone almost black in the dark, that pale ribbon at her ponytail the brightest thing about her. She'd been waiting. Scouting, by the still economy of her — she had the settled quality of someone who'd been in position a while.

"Oh! Hi—" Yuki straightened, ears springing up, hand already coming out. "I'm Yuki, we haven't officially—I'm a huge—"

"Not now." Faith didn't take the hand, but she softened it with the ghost of a smile. "And we've met, in a manner of speaking. You were behind the dumpster on the pharmacy job. You breathe too loudly to hide." She turned to Rin before Yuki could decide whether to be flattered or offended. "The count's wrong. Your broker sold you one man. There are three."

"Three." Yuki's offense evaporated, replaced by wide eyes.

"Three of Kyoya's, together, which they don't do without a reason." Faith's gaze cut past them toward the mouth of the alley, toward whatever waited down the block. "More than I planned for. More than you were told."

"One each," Rin said.

Faith's eyes came back to her, reassessing. "You're that confident."

"She can hold her own." Rin nodded at Yuki, who puffed up. "You obviously can. That's three." She kept the rest to herself — the small, private itch of wondering what it would feel like to take all three alone, to see where her ceiling actually was, a thought she knew better than to say out loud because Ryo would hear about it eventually and would not let it go for a month.

"Why don't we just call your cop friends?" Yuki offered brightly. "You're an investigator, right? Backup. Sirens. The whole—"

Faith let out a slow breath, and for a moment something tired showed under the composure. "The officers who'd answer a call like this aren't equipped to survive it. And the ones equipped to survive it—" she straightened the ribbon at her crown, a small deflecting motion, "—wouldn't spend a second of their evening on it. There's a line in this city between the trouble that gets a response and the trouble that gets buried, and everything I work sits on the wrong side of it." A thin smile. "So it's usually just me. I can summon a crew to clean up afterward. Before and during, I'm a party of one."

"I prefer it this way anyway," Rin said. "Fewer people to keep track of. I'd rather handle it myself."

"Somehow that doesn't surprise me." Faith studied her a moment longer, and there was that appraisal again, warm and collecting. "All right. Three of them, three of—"

Rin was already stepping out of the alley.

"—us," Faith finished, to the empty space where she'd been. She exhaled, almost a laugh. "Or that."

The three men were gathered under a dead awning outside a shuttered mahjong parlor, and they clocked Rin the instant she came out of the dark and walked toward them like she had an appointment.

They didn't know her. She watched them realize it didn't matter. Cheap muscle had an animal sense for the real thing, and whatever they saw in the way she crossed the street — unhurried, hands loose, eyes flat — made all three of them straighten off the wall at once. The green-streaked one she'd been sold reached into his jacket. The biggest cracked his knuckles. And then a shape in red detached from the alley behind her, unhurried, and the temperature of the whole street changed.

"That's the Lotus," the green-streak one said, low. "The red woman. That's the one the boss—"

"Then you already know how your night's going," Faith said pleasantly.

"And the kid?" The big one nodded at Yuki, who bristled at kid.

"Bad news in a small package," Yuki shot back. "Ask your guard how the third kick feels. Oh wait, that was a different guy. You'll find out."

"That didn't mean anything," the big one said, genuinely confused.

"It'll make sense in a second—"

It made sense in a second.

Rin picked hers and moved. The green-streak man got a blade halfway clear before her lead leg drove into his gut and folded him around it; she rode the opening in, snapping a short cross that turned his head and put him staggering back into the awning post. Hers. Clean. She stayed on him.

Faith flowed left to intercept the big one, who'd tried to break toward Rin's fight to help his friend — she simply stepped into his path, redirected his momentum with a turn of her hips, and put him on the back foot with a precision that made Rin's own style look loud.

And Yuki hit the third like a thrown cat, all speed and noise, driving him back with a flurry that had none of Rin's economy and all of Yuki's ferocious, springing energy.

For a few seconds it was going exactly the way Rin wanted.

That was the problem.

She'd put her man on the ropes, dominant, in control, and she leaned into it a beat too far — pressed the advantage with the easy confidence of someone who'd been winning all week — and his desperation found the gap her overconfidence left open. His fist came up off the back foot and caught her flush on the jaw.

The street tilted. White noise. Her legs took an involuntary step sideways that she didn't order them to take, and the taste of copper bloomed, and for one ugly disoriented second the world was underwater.

Faith saw it.

What happened next happened fast. Faith's demeanor changed — the composure sharpened into something with edges. She pivoted off her own opponent and drove a kick into his chest that lifted him clean off his feet and threw him into the shuttered parlor door with a boom that rattled the whole frame. Then she was moving, crossing the distance to Rin's fight in a blur that didn't match anything a human body should do, and her fist came around and buried itself in the green-streak man's ribs.

He left the ground. Not staggered — launched, a grown man thrown backward through the air to crumple against the far curb and stay there.

Silence rang out.

Rin stood swaying slightly, blinking the underwater out of her eyes, one hand drifting to her jaw. Around her, three men were down. She hadn't finished hers. Someone else had finished hers. The feeling was so unfamiliar it took her a moment to name it — she'd been saved, pulled out of a hole by another hand, and she couldn't remember the last time that had happened, couldn't find the shape of how to feel about it.

Faith turned to her, and the edges melted back into warmth. She smiled, small and unbothered, and reached out to tuck a strand of Rin's hair back from the reddening bruise on her jaw with two careful fingers.

"You leaned in," she said gently. "You had him. You wanted to enjoy having him." No scolding in it, only a soft accuracy that landed harder than scolding would have. "It's a beautiful flaw. It'll get you killed."

Rin didn't have an answer. Her jaw hurt and her pride hurt worse and the two careful fingers were still there for half a second too long before Faith withdrew them.

"WOOHOO!"

They both turned. Yuki was perched atop her downed man, breathless, hair wild, ears flat with the fading rush, both fists thrown up over her head. She looked feral and triumphant and about twelve years old in her delight, bouncing where she crouched.

"I got him! Did you see? I kept my guard up on the third kick! I learned it! Rin, did you see—"

Rin saw. She couldn't help but see. And despite the ache in her jaw and the strangeness in her chest, the corner of her mouth pulled toward something warm.

"I saw," she said.

Faith produced her phone, thumb already moving. "Let me get the crew rolling before any of them wake up and make it complicated—"

"Now this," said a new voice, "is a mess."

All three of them went still.

A man was walking up the street toward them, unhurried, hands loose at his sides. He moved like he had all the time in the world and was mildly irritated to be spending it here — a controlled, casual stride with something coiled underneath it. His hair was green, slicked back off his forehead, and his suit was expensive and worn wrong, jacket open, shirt half-untucked, tie discarded, as though he'd dressed with care that morning and then stopped caring by noon. His face wore a small, pleasant, off-kilter smile that didn't reach the flat brightness of his eyes.

"Three of my guys," he went on, conversational, glancing at the bodies without much feeling. "On the ground. On a Friday." He tsked, that pleasant smile never wavering, a thread of something manic humming just under the cool. "You have any idea how annoying it is to replace people? The paperwork. The interviews. The loyalty tests."

He came to a stop under the dead awning, and let them see the thing in his hand.

A knife — though knife undersold it. A long, broad, gleaming blade nearly the length of his forearm, which he held loosely, comfortably, the way Rin held nothing because nothing had ever felt that natural in her grip.

Faith's hand lowered from her phone. Very slowly. Her whole posture had gone to that particular stillness Rin was learning to read as the mask holding under pressure.

"Kyoya," she said.

"In the flesh." He spread his free arm, gracious, the giant blade catching the streetlight as it moved. "And you brought friends to the party, Lotus. How thoughtful. I do so hate drinking alone."
