Chapter Four — The Woman in Red

Dinner was instant curry and a conversation about nothing, which turned out to be Rin's favorite kind.

Mei had a talent for nothing. She could fill twenty minutes with the saga of a vending machine that owed her four hundred yen, a professor's tragic tie, a theory about which dining-hall day was secretly the good one, and never once require Rin to contribute more than a sound. Rin ate slowly and made the sounds and let the small ordinary noise of it settle over her like a coat she was still deciding whether it fit. It was strange, having someone across a table. Strange that she didn't hate it.

Then the light went blue in the window, and it was time.

She rinsed her bowl, dried her hands, and started changing without announcing it — the black clothes, the boots built for rooftops, the jacket that hugged close enough to move in. She had one contract left for the week, a soft mark and a hard neighborhood, and she meant to close it out clean and be back before the courtyard lamps had finished warming up.

Mei didn't ask this time.

That was the first surprise. No are you clubbing, no do you have a secret second life, no sleepy detective work from under the quilt. Mei just watched her dress from the edge of her bed, chin on her knees, quiet in a way that was becoming its own kind of language between them.

The second surprise was that she got up.

She crossed the room in her socks and stopped directly in front of Rin, close, closer than Rin let most people stand, and reached up. For a second Rin held perfectly still, the old wariness firing — but Mei only tugged the collar of the jacket straight where it had folded under, smoothed the shoulder seam flat with two thumbs, and gave the zipper pull a small decisive adjustment like she was sending a kid off to a first day she wasn't sure about.

"There," Mei said softly. "Now you look like you." She looked up. She was very close. Her eyes moved over Rin's face without hurry, and there was worry in them, but no fear, and no questions she was going to make Rin answer. "I don't know what it is you actually do. And I'm not gonna make you tell me. But—good luck. With whatever it is. Come back in one piece, okay?"

The room was quiet. Rin could hear the courtyard, the distant plumbing, her own pulse taking a beat it hadn't asked permission for.

"Okay," she said.

She should have stepped back. She didn't, not right away, and neither did Mei, and the moment stretched a half-second past comfortable and neither of them minded, exactly. Then Mei smiled and dropped back onto her heels, and the spell was an ordinary room again.

"Bring back melon bread if it's open," Mei added, climbing back into her nest. "It's the least a mysterious night job can do."

"No promises."

"That's a yes in Rin."



The neighborhood was the kind that swallowed streetlight. Rin came in high, dropping from a maintenance ladder onto the flat roof of a print shop that overlooked the meeting spot the broker had marked — a dead lot behind a row of failing businesses, chain-link and cracked asphalt and a single working bulb over a loading door.

Her mark was there. She knew his hunch. But he wasn't the picture she'd been sold, a lone frightened man to be plucked and delivered. He stood in a loose knot of others — four, five thugs, cheap muscle by the way they carried themselves, one of them idly swinging a length of pipe like a man who liked the weight of it.

And they were all facing inward. Circling something.

Someone.

Rin lowered herself flat against the roof gravel and looked, and forgot, for a moment, to be a professional.

The woman at the center of the circle was—there wasn't an ordinary word for it. She stood with the unbothered ease of someone who'd chosen to be surrounded, dark hair swept up into a high ponytail bound with a pale ribbon that shouldn't have read as dangerous and somehow did. Red. She wore red, deep and rich against the gray lot, the color catching what little light there was and keeping it. Even from a rooftop, even in the dark, she had the kind of presence that rearranged a space around herself, that made five armed men look like set dressing.

That's her, Rin thought, and something in her chest did an unfamiliar, undignified thing. Yuki hadn't been exaggerating. Yuki had, if anything, undersold it. The woman in red, in the flesh, and Rin found her attention snagging in ways that had nothing to do with the job and took a full breath to drag back.

She made herself listen instead of look.

"—done nothing wrong," one of the thugs was saying, too loud, the bluster of a man who'd already decided to do something stupid.

"That's for a room with better lighting to decide, not me." The woman's voice carried up to the roof without seeming to try, low and smooth and faintly amused. "You'll all come in for questioning. It's simpler than the alternative, and I've had a long week, so let's choose simple." She tilted her head. "I'm an investigator, not your enemy. Yet."

Down in the lot, unseen by Rin, the woman's gaze drifted once—casually, over the print shop roof, across the exact patch of gravel where a girl in black lay flat—and moved on, giving no sign it had found anything. A flicker. Nothing more.

Rin caught none of it. She was busy being impressed. The woman looked ready, looked carved for this, and Rin settled in with a low thrum of anticipation, genuinely curious now, hoping to watch a professional work.

The thug with the pipe swung.

And the woman—

didn't move well enough.

Rin frowned. The pipe caught her across the forearm as she raised it, and she gave ground, and a second man was already inside her guard, driving a fist into her ribs that folded her forward with a sharp gasp. She tried to answer it, a strike that clipped a jaw without much behind it, but a third caught her from the side, a heavy hook that snapped her head and sent her staggering. The pipe came back. It cracked across her shoulders and drove her stumbling into the loading-door wall, and she hit it hard and slid, one hand scrabbling at the brick, and slumped there in her ruined red like something dropped.

They closed in.

Rin was over the parapet before the last of them had raised a boot.

She didn't weigh it, didn't calculate the smart play, didn't do any of the things she prided herself on doing—she simply moved, the drop and the roll and the sprint stitched into one motion, and she came out of the dark at the edge of the bulb's light already among them.

The pipe man turned. She took the pipe. A short check of her palm against his wrist popped his grip, and she caught it out of the air and drove the butt of it into his solar plexus, doubling him, then let it clatter away because she preferred her hands. She turned into the next one and put a straight cross through his guard—a dry crack of knuckle on cheekbone—stepped his stagger, and swept the legs from a third with a low kick that dumped him flat onto the asphalt. The fourth got a grip on her sleeve; she rolled her shoulder, snapped an elbow back into his nose, and felt the wet give of it. The fifth simply looked at the four seconds that had just happened to his friends and decided, wisely, to run.

Then it was quiet, and there were four men on the ground, and Rin stood in the middle of them, breathing steady, not a mark on her.

Which was strange, she thought, turning toward the slumped woman in red. She'd expected to at least share the work. She'd expected the investigator to do more than get folded against a wall by rented muscle. The disappointment surprised her—she'd wanted the woman to be as good as she looked.

"That," said the woman in red, "was even better up close."

Rin went still.

The woman was standing. Not struggling up, not clutching her ribs—standing, smoothly, brushing a bit of grit from her sleeve with two fingers, straightening the pale ribbon at the crown of her ponytail with the unhurried care of someone fixing their appearance in a mirror. There was no blood. No split lip, no bruise blooming, no favoring of the arm the pipe had caught. She looked, if anything, freshly composed, and she was regarding Rin with warm brown eyes and an expression of frank, delighted appraisal.

Rin's stomach dropped a floor.

"You weren't hurt," she said.

"No." The woman smiled, unashamed. "I'm sorry. That was a small deception, and not a kind one." She stepped over a groaning thug without looking down at him, closing the distance with that same rearranging ease. "But I had to know if the stories were true, and stories lie, and a demonstration doesn't. You came off a roof for a stranger you'd never met, without a second's hesitation, and cleared five men in the time it takes most people to decide to be afraid." Her head tilted, ribbon catching the light. "You didn't disappoint."

Rin felt the specific heat of having been played—maneuvered into the open, spent, revealed—and under it a slower unease, because now she was standing in the light with a beaten crew at her feet and no cover story and a woman who'd wanted exactly this.

"You baited me."

"I gave you an opportunity and trusted your character to take it," the woman said, which was the same thing wearing better clothes, and she seemed to know it, and to enjoy that Rin knew it too. She extended a hand—not clumsy like Mei's, not a live wire like the ones Ryo fled from, but offered with a small deliberate grace. "Faith Kiryu."

Rin looked at the hand. Looked at the woman. Took it.

"Rin. Aizawa."

"I know." Faith's grip was cool and certain and let go at exactly the right moment. "You're the one who put two of the wrong people's men on the ground last night, near a dead pharmacy. Word travels, in the circles I've been listening to." At Rin's flicker, she added, gently, "I've been working the brokers. Following the money and the muscle up the chain. Your name kept turning up in the ledgers—the girl who finishes fast. I wanted to see her finish."

"You're really an investigator."

"Among other things." Faith glanced down at the men beginning to stir, unconcerned. "Which is why I'll tell you, as someone who has read a great many files that end the way this work tends to end: bounty hunting is a short career for most who try it. Dangerous. Lonely. You're good—better than good—but good is what the last several were called too, in the ledgers, past tense." She said it without cruelty, almost fondly, a warning dressed as small talk.

Rin studied her. The composure. The absence of a single scratch. The way she'd taken a pipe across the shoulders and a fist to the ribs and now stood there arranging her ribbon like she'd stepped out of a car.

"Those hits weren't fake," Rin said slowly. "The pipe connected. I heard it. That should've broken something."

Something moved behind Faith's eyes—a door easing shut rather than open—and for just a moment the warmth held very, very still. Then it came back, smooth as water closing over a stone.

"No," she agreed lightly. "It should have."

"How—"

"I'm a little harder to break than I let on." Faith's smile turned, gained a private edge. "That's all you get tonight. A woman should keep something back on a first meeting; it gives her a reason to see you again." She winked—unhurried, deliberate, a thing done at Rin rather than merely near her—and Rin felt her own face threaten to do something and killed it. "Thank you, truly, for the rescue. Even if you were rescuing someone who didn't strictly need it. The gesture is what counts, and yours was very convincing."

Rin remembered, belatedly, the job. "I need to call this in. The mark—"

"Already handled." Faith produced a slim phone, turned it so Rin could see it dark and idle in her palm. "I called it through while you were being modest. Your broker has his package, and you—" A small nod toward Rin's own pocket. "—have your fee. Check, if you like. I made sure they didn't shave it."

Rin checked. The number sat there in her banking app, transferred, complete—larger than the contract had promised, in fact, rounded up by a hand that had wanted her to notice. And despite herself, despite the deception and the exposure and the whole strange evening, a small warmth she didn't examine loosened in her chest. Paid, clean, done, and someone had made sure she wasn't cheated.

"Why," she said.

"Because I like the look of you, and it costs me nothing to be generous with money that isn't mine." Faith was already stepping back toward the shadows at the lot's edge, moving in that way that owned whatever ground it crossed. "Go home, Rin Aizawa. It's late, and you have class." She paused at the dark's edge, red going nearly black in it. "We'll be running into each other, I expect. We seem to keep the same hours and want the same people caught. Common interests make for easy company."

Rin nodded, once.

And then Faith was gone—not dramatically, not with any flourish, simply not there between one glance and the next, the way only certain people could leave a place.

Rin stood alone among the groaning men, the single bulb buzzing overhead, and let out a slow breath she hadn't known she'd been holding.



Neither of them saw the figure on the far roof.

She had watched the whole of it from the deep shadow of a stairwell housing three buildings over—the fake beating, the rescue, the handshake, the wink. She stood with her arms loosely folded, a blonde in a place no professor had any business being, and she wound a strand of pale hair slowly around one finger, over and over, thinking.

When she'd first noticed the Aizawa girl it had been idle curiosity, a clever answer in a lecture hall, nothing more. This was more. This was a girl who went off rooftops for strangers and cleared armed men without breathing hard and had just been circled by the woman in red without understanding, yet, what that meant.

The blonde smiled to herself in the dark. Her eyes, pale and unremarkable in daylight, caught the city's low glow now and gave it back the wrong color—a faint, patient red.

"Rin Aizawa," she murmured, tasting it, and unwound the strand of hair, and was gone before either woman below thought to look up.



Room 204 still had a lamp on.

Mei was awake, sitting up against the wall with the quilt around her shoulders and a textbook open and unread in her lap, and the relief that crossed her face when the door opened was so undisguised it caught Rin off guard.

"You're back." Mei let out a breath. "Okay. Good. I wasn't— I mean I was a little— you were gone kind of long, and I did the math on how long stocking shelves usually takes, and—" She stopped herself, then reached over to her desk, peeled a fresh sticky note off the pad, and wrote something on it with great ceremony. She held it up.

It read: "Shelf fell on me" — excuse #4.

"I'm keeping a list," Mei announced. "Of the excuses. For science. This one and the box one are already fighting for first place."

"It's a dangerous store," Rin said, toeing off her boots.

"Excuse four-point-one," Mei muttered, adding a mark, and stuck the note to the wall above her desk beside the others, a small growing constellation of Rin's fictions. But she was smiling as she did it, and the smile had no edge in it, only that fond, worried warmth she seemed to make more of than any one person should be able to.

Rin sat on her own bed and watched her roommate curate a little museum of her lies with complete affection and no intention of ever calling her on them, and felt it land somewhere she usually kept locked—that Mei worried, that Mei waited up, that Mei would rather build a joke around not-knowing than push at a door Rin wasn't ready to open.

"I couldn't get the melon bread," Rin offered, which was as close to an apology for the whole evening as she had.

Mei clutched her chest, wounded. "Excuse five."

And Rin—who had been baited off a roof, exposed, out-maneuvered, and left oddly lighter for it, who had a paid contract and a woman in red now written somewhere permanent in her week—lay back in the lamp-lit warm of a room that was half someone else's, and let herself, just for tonight, feel wanted.
