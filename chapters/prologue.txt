Prologue — Last Night in the Old City

The warehouse smelled like rust and old rain.

Rin Aizawa stood in the dark between two shipping racks and counted breathing. Not hers — she'd stopped noticing hers a long time ago. Theirs. Four men, maybe five, spread loose across the loading floor below, their voices bouncing flat off corrugated steel. The one she wanted sat on an overturned crate in the middle of them, a soft man with soft hands who owed a broker named Endo more money than his life was apparently worth. The contract had a photo, a name she didn't bother memorizing, and a number at the bottom that was smaller than she'd hoped.

Enough to get by. It was always enough to get by. Never enough to matter.

She let the thought go and let the stillness take its place.

This was the part she didn't tell anyone about, because there was no one to tell. Down in the dark, with her pulse finally loud enough to hear, the static that followed her through every ordinary hour of her life went quiet. The world sharpened to edges. She could see the loose board under the near man's boot, the pipe leaning against the rack within reach of the big one, the exit sign flickering red over the side door the soft man would run for the instant things went wrong.

He'd run right. They always ran toward the light.

She stepped out of the dark.

The first man saw her too late — a girl in black, unhurried, hands low and open like she'd wandered in by accident. He opened his mouth to say something, and Rin's lead leg snapped up into a teep that folded him backward over the crate behind him with a sound like a dropped sack. She was already moving before he landed.

The second one reached for the pipe. She let him have it. She wanted him to feel the weight of it, the confidence of it, before she took the confidence away — she slipped inside the arc of his first wild swing, close enough to smell the beer on him, and drove a short cross down the center of his face. Snap-crack. His head went back. The pipe rang off the concrete a full second after he did.

"Get her —"

She didn't wait for the rest of the sentence.

The big one came in fast for his size, arms wide, trying to swallow her up in a bear-grip. She pivoted off his line and let his own weight carry him past, then pushed off the steel rack behind her — a hard shove of her boot against cold metal — and used the rebound to spin through his blind side. The elbow caught him behind the ear on the way around. Thwock. He dropped to a knee, blinking at nothing, and Rin was gone before he could decide whether he was still in the fight.

He wasn't. He just didn't know it yet.

The fourth man got a hand on her — a fist of her collar, a grip, real fear behind it. She felt the seam of her jacket bite into the back of her neck and exhaled once, sharp through the nose, more annoyed than hurt. She snapped her forehead forward into the bridge of his nose. The clinch broke on a wet crunch and a howl, and she stepped off him without watching him fall.

Four seconds. Maybe five.

She rolled her shoulders once, testing. The collar-grab would leave a red mark, nothing more. Minimal. Clean. Her knuckles ached in that dull familiar way, and there was a scuff of grit on her cheek she'd feel later in the shower. That was the whole tally.

The soft man was already running. Toward the side door. Toward the light.

Rin sighed, quietly, and followed at a walk.

He made it three steps into the alley before his own panic tangled his feet. By the time he'd scrambled up, she was standing over him, hands in her pockets, the flickering exit sign painting them both intermittent red. She didn't say anything. She'd learned that silence did the work talking never could — the man filled it himself, babbling numbers and names and promises, offering her more than the bounty was worth to let him go.

"I don't set the price," she said. Flat. Bored. "I just collect it."

She got him to his feet and got the broker's people on the phone and got, in the end, the smaller-than-she'd-hoped number transferred to an account under a name that wasn't quite hers. The whole exchange took longer than the fight. It always did.



Her apartment was a single room over a shuttered laundromat, and she stood in the middle of it now with the lights off, listening to it be empty.

She'd already packed. That was the strange part — the packing had taken an afternoon, because there'd been so little to pack. Two boxes and a duffel. Some clothes, most of them black. A rice cooker with a cracked lid. A framed photo she kept face-down in a drawer and had not turned over while packing. The bed was stripped, the mattress bare and accusing in the dark.

Tomorrow she'd carry all of it across the city to a dorm room with a stranger's name on the other bed.

Rin sat on the bare mattress and let the static come back. Now that the danger was hours behind her, it returned the way it always did — a low hum under everything, the feeling of watching her own life through smudged glass. She turned her hands over and looked at the bruising starting to bloom across two knuckles.

How does a person end up here, she thought, without much feeling in it. Eighteen years old. An apartment she never decorated because she never planned to stay. A phone full of brokers and no friends. A body that only felt like hers when someone was trying to break it.

She knew the shape of the answer and didn't want to trace it tonight. It had edges she'd rather leave in the dark, same as everything else.

College. That was the plan now — or someone's plan, dressed up as hers. A mostly-girls academy across the city, a real dorm, a schedule, the outline of an ordinary life she was supposed to step into like a borrowed coat. She didn't know yet if she'd fit it. She suspected not. But she'd said yes, because saying yes was easier than explaining why the idea of a normal room with a normal roommate made something in her chest go tight and cold.

Maybe it would be quiet there. Maybe quiet was the point.

Maybe, she thought, lying back on the bare mattress with her boots still on, she'd finally find out what she was supposed to do when nothing was trying to kill her.

She didn't sleep much. She rarely did.



The academy looked like a brochure.

Rin stood at the top of the entrance steps the next morning, a box under one arm, and squinted at more brightness than she was used to. Sun on pale stone. Cherry trees past their bloom, dropping the last of their petals across walkways full of girls — and it was, as far as she could tell, mostly girls, streams of them in fresh uniforms moving in the easy clusters of people who already belonged somewhere. A few boys threaded through the crowd, outnumbered and clearly aware of it.

And then there was Ryo.

He stuck out like a splinter. Everyone else was pressed and buttoned and bright-eyed with first-day nerves; Ryo Kurosaki was leaning against a pillar near the doors with his collar open, his tie loose enough to be a rumor, and the specific slouch of a man who had already decided the day was beneath him. His dark hair did whatever it wanted. He was, somehow, already holding a can of coffee.

He spotted her and lifted the can two inches in greeting.

"You look like you slept great," he said, as she reached him.

"I look like I slept," she said.

"That's the joke." He fell into step beside her without being asked, hands in his pockets, unhurried in a way that matched hers so exactly it had taken them years to notice it was a kind of friendship. "You had a job last night."

It wasn't a question. Ryo didn't ask her things he already knew. He'd known about the bounties for two years now, had gone from horrified to furious to worried to, finally, this — a flat, permanent acceptance that lived somewhere behind his eyes and never quite left.

"It was easy," she said.

"They're always easy, right up until they're not." He said it lightly, but she caught the weight underneath, the same weight that was always there. He took a sip of coffee. "You'd tell me. If one wasn't."

"No," she said, honestly.

"Yeah." He nodded, unsurprised. "Figured."

The opening ceremony had already ended — they'd both skipped the tail of it, drifting out with the crowd rather than sitting through the speeches, and no one had stopped them. Now the first-years were being funneled toward long tables in the entrance hall where upperclassmen with clipboards handed out printed schedules and dorm assignments and thin welcome folders that Rin already knew she'd never open.

She joined a line. Ryo joined it beside her, technically not in it, entirely unbothered by the distinction.

"You know this is a girls' school, basically," she said, watching him.

"I noticed." He looked mildly around at the sea of uniforms with the serenity of a man who had genuinely never in his life cared what a room thought of him. "It's fine. I'm here for one person."

She didn't answer that. She didn't have to. It was the truest thing he'd say all day and they both let it sit there, uncommented, the way they let most true things sit.

The line moved. When she reached the table, a girl with a clipboard found her name, ran a finger down a column, and handed her a schedule still warm from the printer and a small card with a room number on it.

West Dormitory. Room 204.

Underneath the number, in smaller print, a second name. Her roommate. Rin read it once, felt nothing in particular about it, and folded the card into her pocket.

"Well?" Ryo asked.

"West dorm. Two-oh-four." She showed him the schedule instead of the card. "Half of these classes I've never heard of."

"That's college." He drained the last of the coffee and looked around for somewhere to put the can, found nowhere, and kept it. "You want help hauling boxes?"

"I've got it."

"I know you've got it." He said it the way he said most things to her — dry, patient, meaning something underneath. I know you've got it. I'm offering anyway. I'm always going to offer anyway. "Text me when you're settled. Or don't. But then I'll come find you, and you'll hate that more."

The corner of her mouth moved. Not a smile. Adjacent to one.

"See you, Ryo."

"See you, Rin." He pushed off the pillar he'd migrated back to and ambled toward the gates against the current of arriving students, a splinter working its way loose, and did not look back — because he never worried she'd disappear. He was the one person in the world who simply trusted she'd still be there tomorrow.

Rin stood alone a moment in the thinning crowd, box under her arm, sun too bright, the static already rising in the quiet.

Then she shouldered the weight, checked the card in her pocket one more time — West Dormitory, Room 204 — and went to find the room where the rest of it would start.
